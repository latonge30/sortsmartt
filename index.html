How Recycling Works

Recycling works as a chain of connected steps. An item must be accepted, collected, sorted, processed, and sent to a facility that can use the recovered material.

1

Identify the material

Determine what the item is made from. A plastic bottle, plastic bag, and foam container may require different disposal methods.

2

Check your local rules

Review your county and community's recycling information. Confirm whether an item is accepted curbside, at a drop-off location, or through a special collection program.

3

Prepare the item

Empty containers, remove leftover food, and follow local instructions for rinsing, lids, cardboard, and other materials.

4

Collection and transportation

Accepted materials are collected by a recycling service or taken to a designated drop-off location.

5

Sorting and processing

Facilities separate materials using equipment and sometimes manual sorting. Recovered materials are processed for manufacturing.

6

Manufacturing and reuse

Processed paper, metals, glass, and suitable plastics can become raw materials for new products.

Why contamination matters: Food residue, liquids, plastic bags, hazardous waste, and unaccepted items can interfere with sorting or reduce the quality of recovered materials.

Know what goes where

Waste Types and General Recycling Rules

These are general guidelines for handling everyday materials. Recycling availability and preparation requirements vary by community, so always confirm local instructions before using a curbside bin or drop-off service.

General rule: An item being made from a recyclable material does not mean every recycling program accepts it. Check the exact item, not just its material or symbol.
📄
Paper products

Paper and Cardboard

General rule: Keep accepted paper clean and dry. Flatten cardboard boxes if required.

Newspapers, office paper, and corrugated cardboard are commonly recyclable. Greasy paper, heavily food-soiled cardboard, tissues, and coated paper may not be accepted.

Avoid: Mixing wet or greasy paper with clean recyclables.

🧴
Plastic

Plastic Containers

General rule: Check whether the specific rigid plastic container is accepted. Empty it and follow local instructions for lids.

Bottles, jugs, and tubs may be accepted. Plastic bags, film, foam, toys, and mixed-material packaging often require different disposal options.

Avoid: Assuming the resin number guarantees curbside acceptance.

🍾
Glass

Glass Bottles and Jars

General rule: Check whether container glass is accepted curbside or through a dedicated drop-off site.

Empty beverage bottles and food jars may be accepted. Drinking glasses, mirrors, window glass, ceramics, and heat-resistant cookware generally do not belong with ordinary container glass.

Avoid: Mixing incompatible glass types.

🥫
Metal

Aluminum and Steel Cans

General rule: Empty accepted food and beverage cans. Follow local directions for lids and sharp edges.

Metal scrap, appliances, propane cylinders, and pressurized containers may require special handling.

Avoid: Placing pressurized or hazardous containers in ordinary recycling bins.

🔌
Electronic waste

Electronics

General rule: Use an electronics recycler, manufacturer take-back program, or designated collection event.

Phones, computers, tablets, and cords can contain recoverable materials. Before handing over a device, back up your files and erase personal information as appropriate.

Avoid: Putting electronics in ordinary curbside bins unless explicitly accepted.

🍂
Organic material

Food Scraps and Yard Waste

General rule: Check whether your community offers composting, organics collection, or yard-waste pickup.

Leaves, grass, and suitable food scraps can be composted. Meat, dairy, oils, pet waste, and compostable packaging depend on the type of composting system.

Avoid: Assuming all compostable packaging belongs in a backyard compost pile.

🔋
Special collection

Batteries and Light Bulbs

General rule: Follow battery-specific collection instructions. Some batteries require terminal protection to reduce fire risk.

Rechargeable batteries, lithium batteries, and fluorescent lamps may need designated collection options.

Avoid: Placing loose batteries in ordinary recycling or trash without checking the appropriate guidance.

👕
Reuse first

Clothing and Textiles

General rule: Donate clean, usable clothing or use a textile collection program that accepts your items.

Some organizations accept worn textiles for specialized processing. Acceptance requirements vary.

Avoid: Donating wet, moldy, or dirty items to programs that cannot process them.

⚠️
Handle carefully

Household Hazardous Waste

General rule: Check local disposal guidance for paints, solvents, pesticides, automotive fluids, and other potentially hazardous products.

Keep products in their original labeled containers when safe to do so. Contact the collection program before transporting questionable materials.

Avoid: Mixing unknown chemicals or pouring them into drains, storm sewers, or onto the ground.

🥡
Check carefully

Food Packaging and Cartons

General rule: Empty packaging and check the rules for the exact package type.

Cartons, takeout containers, coated paper, and mixed-material packages can be difficult to process. Food residue and different layers may affect acceptance.

Avoid: Assuming that all paper-looking or plastic-looking packaging is recyclable.

🛍️
Usually separate

Plastic Bags and Film

General rule: Keep plastic bags and film out of curbside recycling unless your program explicitly accepts them.

Some grocery stores and specialized programs collect clean, dry plastic film. Accepted bag and film types vary.

Avoid: Putting bagged recyclables into a curbside bin without checking local instructions.

🪑
Large items

Furniture and Appliances

General rule: Consider donation, repair, resale, bulk pickup, or a designated recycling facility.

Appliances may contain refrigerants or other components that require special handling. Pickup and drop-off rules vary.

Avoid: Leaving large items beside a recycling bin without arranging proper collection.

Quick rules to remember

1. Reduce and reuse

Prevent waste and reuse usable products before recycling.

2. Keep recyclables clean

Empty containers and follow the service's preparation rules.

3. Do not guess

Check the exact material and item in the local guidelines.

4. Separate special waste

Use the correct collection program for batteries, chemicals, electronics, and other special items.

Understand the numbers on plastic

Plastic Recycling Numbers #1–#7

Many plastic products have a number inside a triangular symbol made of chasing arrows. This is a resin identification code: it identifies the main type of plastic used to make the item. It does not guarantee that the item is recyclable or accepted by your local recycling program.

Important: The number identifies the plastic resin, not how many times an item has been recycled and not whether your curbside service accepts it. Shape, additives, color, contamination, local equipment, and available markets can all affect recyclability.
#1
PET or PETE

Polyethylene Terephthalate

Common items: Water and soda bottles, some food jars, and clear beverage containers.

General guidance: Many recycling programs accept common PET beverage bottles. Empty them and follow local directions for caps and labels.

Remember: Not every PET tray, clamshell, or other package is accepted.

#2
HDPE

High-Density Polyethylene

Common items: Milk jugs, detergent bottles, shampoo bottles, and some household-product containers.

General guidance: Many programs accept common HDPE bottles and jugs when emptied and prepared properly.

Remember: Acceptance of other HDPE objects and containers can differ.

#3
PVC or V

Polyvinyl Chloride

Common items: Certain pipes, construction materials, vinyl products, and some packaging.

General guidance: PVC is generally not accepted in ordinary household curbside container recycling. Look for a suitable specialized program when available.

Remember: Do not put PVC pipes or building materials in a bin just because they carry a number.

#4
LDPE

Low-Density Polyethylene

Common items: Squeezable bottles, some lids, plastic film, and shopping or bread bags.

General guidance: Some rigid LDPE products may be accepted by particular programs. Plastic film and bags usually require separate collection.

Remember: Keep bags and loose film out of curbside recycling unless specifically accepted.

#5
PP

Polypropylene

Common items: Yogurt tubs, food containers, some bottle caps, and reusable food-storage products.

General guidance: Some communities accept rigid polypropylene tubs and containers.

Remember: Acceptance depends on the exact item and local program, not the number alone.

#6
PS

Polystyrene

Common items: Foam cups, foam packaging, protective packing pieces, and some rigid disposable products.

General guidance: Many curbside programs do not accept foam polystyrene. A specialized drop-off option may exist in some areas.

Remember: Check before placing foam packaging or disposable cups in the recycling bin.

#7
Other / Mixed

Other Plastics

Common items: Certain reusable bottles, multi-layer packaging, specialty plastics, and products made from more than one type of resin.

General guidance: This category covers plastics that do not fit neatly into codes #1–#6. Recycling options vary widely.

Remember: Check the product and local acceptance rules instead of assuming it is recyclable.

What do the plastic numbers mean?

They identify the resin. The codes help distinguish common plastic types, such as PET, HDPE, and PP.

They do not guarantee recycling. A facility must have the equipment and market to recover a particular item.

They do not indicate safety. A resin code alone is not a complete statement about whether an item is safe for a particular use.

They do not replace local rules. The same type of plastic can be accepted in one program and rejected in another.

How to sort plastic correctly

1

Find the code

Look for the number on the container or its packaging. If there is no number, do not guess the resin type.

2

Identify the actual item

A bottle, foam cup, plastic bag, and plastic toy can require different handling even when their material codes are related.

3

Check the local program

Confirm that the exact item is accepted curbside or at a designated drop-off location.

4

Prepare it as instructed

Empty the container and follow directions about lids, rinsing, labels, and other components.

Best rule of thumb: Identify the plastic, identify the item, and then check the local recycling rules. Never rely on the number alone.
